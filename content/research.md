<div class="research-title">Bringing Back What Should Have Never Left: Class Relations, Class Structure, and Political Participation</div>
<div class="research-summary">How exploitative and antagonistic class relations shape political participation across 33 countries.</div>
<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>While the link between social class and politics is well established in sociology, much research has focused on describing differences in political behavior that arise from class structure rather than the mechanisms behind the structuration of class structure itself. This article addresses this gap by examining the mechanisms that structure class structure and how it shapes political participation. By exploring the neo-Marxist tradition, which treats exploitative and antagonistic class relations as key defining variables of class structure, this article suggests that exploitation acts as a barrier to political engagement and that antagonism between classes helps explain the gap in participation levels. Drawing on data from the ISSP 2014 Survey (N = 24,199) across 33 countries and using multivariate regressions and matching, I find that political participation declines at lower positions in the class structure and that the largest difference in political activity is between the capitalist class and the traditional proletariat, the two ends of the structure.</p>
</details>

<div class="research-title">For an Economic Sociology of Capitalism</div>
<div class="research-summary">A review of how economic sociology has engaged with capitalism, and a case for bringing back the study of accumulation processes from an historical perspective.</div>
<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>This article reviews the main debates that have defined economic sociology as a field, focusing on how they have addressed capitalism as an economic system. I suggest that economic sociology has successfully challenged economics, especially orthodox economics and the rational choice model, by showing that the economy is embedded in, and depends on, social structures. In doing so, however, it has left behind what I call the "historical economic sociology of capitalism", a tradition that seeks to understand not only the social within the economic, but above all the patterns of capitalist accumulation from an historical perspective. The call, then, is to study capitalist processes of accumulation and production and include them in the main corpus of economic sociology.</p>
</details>

<div class="research-card">
<div class="research-title">Class antagonism in hard times: class position, local economic shocks and distributive conflict in Chile, 2016-2023</div>
<div class="research-summary">How economic shocks affect conflicts over distribution in Chile.</div>
<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>Class conflict is often described as latent in ordinary times and as becoming visible when economic conditions worsen. I test this claim with the Chilean Longitudinal Social Survey (ELSOC), a seven-wave panel from 2016 to 2023. The panel spans the October 2019 social uprising, the COVID-19 recession, the cost-of-living crisis that followed, and two constitutional processes. I measure class position before these events, built from employment relations, firm size, supervision, and occupation. The outcomes are distributive attitudes that ELSOC asks in every wave: perceived and fair pay gaps between managers and unskilled workers, the view that income differences are too large, trust in unions and in private firms, and support for disruptive labor protest. An event-study difference-in-differences design with individual and wave fixed effects estimates whether class gaps in these attitudes widen after each crisis, using the pre-2019 waves to test for parallel trends. I then link respondents to local economic conditions, namely regional unemployment at the time of the interview and commune (comuna) poverty estimates, to ask whether class gaps widen most where the economic shock was largest.</p>
</details>
